What you learn

Every part has a price.
Every result has a reason.

There is no single perfect answer, just trade-offs. A bigger antenna sends more data but costs mass, power and fuel. A lower orbit is cheaper to reach but harder to talk through. The game makes you feel those trade-offs, then explains them.

  • Decide. Choose parts, orbits, landing sites and every move, with live mass, cost and power meters.
  • Forecast. A mission analyst calculates the outcome of each option before you choose, shows the odds as a decision tree, and offers designs that really work.
  • Test. A go / no-go board blocks a launch that is too heavy, over budget, under-powered or too low.
  • Survive the surprises. Solar storms, dust, stuck wheels and heater faults ask for a decision under risk.
  • Debrief. A score, the reasons behind it and real missions that faced the same problem.

Choose your world

The Moon first. Then Mars, and beyond.

Every world is drawn from a NASA global map. Switch the Moon and Mars between imagery and laser-altimeter elevation in Mission Control, and the rover flies on real elevation data.

  • Main campaign

    Moon

    Shackleton crater rim, south pole. Water ice hides in craters that never see the Sun.

  • Bonus campaign

    Mars

    Jezero crater, an ancient river delta where Perseverance collects samples.

  • Orbit challenge

    Mercury

    Closest to the Sun and the hardest to reach: you need a lot of fuel to slow down.

  • Orbit challenge

    Ceres

    A dwarf planet with salty bright spots. Gravity is so weak that orbits are slow and gentle.

  • Orbit challenge

    Vesta

    A giant asteroid with a basin 500 km wide. The map colours show height, measured by Dawn’s camera.

The real hardware

Real NASA spacecraft. Spin them around.

The rockets, satellites, rovers and dishes in the game are modelled on real NASA hardware, using NASA’s open 3D models. When you pick an antenna or a launcher, the model changes with your choice.

The team

Team Cat on Moon, Budapest.

Jeongha, David and Emad built this for the NASA Space Apps Challenge 2026, “Space Mission Design Game”. The idea came from a brainstorm by the three of us, and Mission 3’s signal-encoding game is based on Jeongha’s design.

Some code was written with the help of an AI coding assistant, as allowed by the Space Apps rules. The idea, missions and design decisions are the team’s.
